Production

Christopher Nolan wrote the screenplay for Inception over a period of roughly ten years, originally
conceiving it as a horror story about "dream stealers" before reworking it into a heist film built
around the mechanics of shared dreaming. He has said the idea was partly inspired by the experience
of lucid dreaming and by the way architecture can feel unstable and shifting in a dream state.
Nolan first pitched the project to Warner Bros. in 2001 after the release of Memento, but felt the
script was not ready and continued to develop it while making The Dark Knight trilogy.

Principal photography began in June 2009 and took place across six countries, including Japan,
France, the United Kingdom, Morocco, Canada, and the United States. The rotating hallway fight
sequence, in which actor Joseph Gordon-Levitt battles a training projection in zero gravity, was
filmed practically inside a custom-built rotating corridor set rather than relying on CGI. The set
was constructed as a fully rotating drum that could spin a full 360 degrees, and the production
crew operated cameras that were bolted to the rotating structure itself so the geometry of the
fight would read correctly on screen.

The Paris "folding city" sequence, where the dreamscape bends the street grid back on itself, was
achieved with a combination of practical miniatures and digital effects supervised by Paul Franklin
at Double Negative. Nolan insisted on shooting as much as possible in-camera, reserving CGI mainly
for compositing and for the folding of the city itself, which could not be done practically at full
scale.

Hans Zimmer's score makes prominent use of a heavily slowed-down and processed rendition of Édith
Piaf's "Non, je ne regrette rien," which functions in the story as the audio cue used to wake the
dreamers. Zimmer has said he slowed the recording down by a factor of roughly 8x to derive the
brass motif that recurs throughout the score, a production choice that ties the sound design
directly to the film's central plot mechanic.

Casting

Leonardo DiCaprio was attached to star as Dom Cobb before Nolan had finished the script, and Nolan
has credited DiCaprio's early commitment with helping get the project greenlit at a $160 million
budget, unusually large for an original, non-franchise screenplay. Marion Cotillard was cast as
Mal after Nolan saw her performance in La Vie en Rose; the casting was finalized before Cotillard
had read the full script, on the strength of a single in-person meeting with Nolan. Cillian Murphy,
who had appeared in Nolan's Batman films, was cast as Robert Fischer, marking his fourth
collaboration with the director. Tom Hardy was cast as Eames in part because Nolan wanted an actor
who could plausibly play a character who impersonates other characters within the film.
