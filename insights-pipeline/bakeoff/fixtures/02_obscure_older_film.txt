Background

Detour is a 1945 American film noir directed by Edgar G. Ulmer, produced on a poverty-row budget at
Producers Releasing Corporation, one of the smallest and least prestigious studios operating in
Hollywood at the time. The film was shot in approximately six days, a schedule so compressed that
Ulmer reportedly could not afford more than one or two takes of most scenes, and this constraint is
frequently cited by historians as shaping the film's stark, almost accidental visual style rather
than any coordinated artistic choice.

The screenplay was adapted by Martin Goldsmith from his own 1939 novel of the same name, though
Ulmer and an uncredited writer, Martin Mooney, reworked substantial portions of the dialogue during
production. Budget estimates for the finished film range from about $20,000 to $100,000 depending
on the source, reflecting how little verified financial documentation survives from PRC, a studio
whose records were largely lost after it ceased operating in 1947.

Tom Neal stars as Al Roberts, a nightclub pianist who hitchhikes across the country and becomes
entangled in a series of increasingly desperate circumstances after a chance encounter with a
dying stranger. Ann Savage co-stars as Vera, a hitchhiker whose hostile, abrasive performance has
become one of the most discussed elements of the film in later critical reassessment. Neal and
Savage reportedly did not get along during filming, and some critics have suggested this real-life
friction is visible in the tension of their scenes together, though this claim is difficult to
verify and is often repeated without a clear original source.

For decades Detour was considered a minor B-picture and was not widely seen outside of revival
theaters and television broadcasts of poor-quality prints, since PRC's negatives were not well
preserved. Its reputation grew substantially after a 1992 preservation and restoration by the
UCLA Film and Television Archive, and it became the first film noir to be added to the United
States National Film Registry in 1992, a fact often cited as evidence of its rehabilitated critical
standing relative to its original poverty-row release.
